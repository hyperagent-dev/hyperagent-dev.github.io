Hyperagent Review (2026): What It Does and What It Costs

An independent look at hyperagent: what the fleet actually delivers, what those per-artifact price tags mean, and when a one-off generator beats it.

The short versionHyperagent suits teams with recurring, format-heavy output who can write a tight brief and want the result delivered into Slack on a schedule. It is a poor fit for someone who needs one landing page this afternoon, because the fleet, the command center and the scheduling are overhead that job does not need. The caveat is pricing: the homepage shows per-artifact costs on sample cards but no plan table, so model your budget on runtime rather than on a monthly number. If the job is a single site or app you want to own as files, Begin.sh is the cheaper shape.

What the homepage actually promises

Hyperagent pitches a fleet of agents you brief once. The site says each agent comes with its own computing environment, real browsers and real shells, plus access to the tools your team already uses. Output is framed as deliverables rather than chat: websites, video, slides, documents and dashboards, with a specific note that the HTML renders live instead of being pasted into a doc. Delivery happens where you already work, through a Slack mention, Telegram, a webhook or a schedule. The other claim worth flagging is persistence. The copy says agents keep delivered work current as your situation changes, and that they generate new memories and skills as they go. None of that is verifiable from outside an account, but it is a clear description of the product shape, and it tells you the company is selling ongoing output rather than a single generation.

The artifact cards say more than the copy does

Each sample on the front page carries a runtime and a dollar figure: an out-of-home campaign at eight minutes and $6.41, a cinematic real estate video at twenty-nine minutes and $24.79, a hiring command center at twenty-two minutes and $14.18, a brand identity pass at fifteen minutes and $3.88. Those numbers are the closest thing to pricing anywhere on the page. Read them as unit costs for one finished artifact, not as a plan price, because no subscription table is published. Two things follow. Cost tracks how long an agent works, which means a vague brief is literally more expensive than a precise one. And video is the outlier in the set shown, costing roughly six times the brand job, so it is the format to test last rather than first.

Where this billing model bites

Work billed by runtime rewards people who already know what good output looks like. Write a brief that carries the constraints, the references and the acceptance criteria, and you get something usable in one pass. Skip that, and you pay for the agent to explore, which is the slow part. Anyone who has managed contractors will recognise the pattern. Teams that keep re-briefing will find the homepage figures optimistic, because those samples were presumably run by people who knew exactly what they wanted before they started. Budget a few throwaway runs while you learn the brief format, and decide up front which categories of work are worth agent minutes at all. A weekly market brief probably is. A logo you will redraw by hand anyway probably is not.

Who should look elsewhere

Anyone whose real need is one small artifact will feel the overhead. A fleet, a command center, Slack wiring and scheduled runs are built for a team with recurring output, not for a solo founder who wants a page shipped today. If your task is narrow and file-shaped, a single-purpose generator is faster to reason about and cheaper to abandon. Begin.sh sits in that narrower slot: describe a site or an app, or point it at a URL you want cloned, and download the source as a zip. There is no fleet to manage and nothing hosted for you, which is a limitation when you want work maintained over months, and an advantage when you want the files and nothing else in the loop.

Questions the site leaves open

No pricing page, no free tier, no rate limit, no model list and no data retention policy appear on the homepage, so treat every number here as coming from sample cards rather than from a contract. The site also does not say what happens to credentials you hand an agent that has shell access, which is the first question any security reviewer will ask. Access terms and limits on young products like this move month to month. Before committing a budget, read the current terms and privacy pages on hyperagent.com, and ask support directly where agent environments run, how long artifacts and logs are kept, and whether hosted pages survive a cancelled plan.

What you actually get

Deliverables, not transcripts

Output is framed as finished files: sites, video, slides, documents and dashboards. The websites claim is specific, that the HTML renders live rather than arriving as code pasted into a document.

Runtime and cost on show

Sample artifacts list minutes and dollars side by side, from $3.88 for a brand pass to $24.79 for a real estate video. That is unusual transparency for an agent product, even without a plan table.

Lives in your chat tools

Work arrives through Slack, Telegram, a webhook or a schedule instead of a dashboard you have to remember to open. Valuable for a team, close to irrelevant if you work alone.

Visible execution trail

The platform section says you can watch every search and decision as an artifact is built. Useful for catching a bad brief at minute two rather than paying for the whole run.

How to trial it without burning money

  1. Write the brief first
    Draft the assignment in a text file before you open the product. Include constraints, references and a definition of finished. Cost tracks runtime, so vagueness is billable.
  2. Start with a cheap format
    Pick a document or a dashboard for the first run rather than video. On the sample cards, video is the most expensive category by a wide margin.
  3. Watch the run live
    Follow the searches and decisions while they happen. Stop and re-brief early if the agent is solving the wrong problem, instead of reviewing only the final artifact.
  4. Benchmark against a narrow tool
    Push the same brief through a single-purpose generator such as Begin.sh. If a downloadable zip covers the job, the fleet overhead is not buying you anything.

FAQ

What does hyperagent cost?

No plan table appears on the homepage. What it does show is per-artifact figures beside its examples, from $3.88 for a brand identity pass to $24.79 for a cinematic real estate video, each with a runtime in minutes. Treat those as illustrative unit costs and check the official site for current pricing before you commit a budget.

Is this the official Hyperagent site?

No. This is an independent review page written by a practitioner, with no affiliation to the company. Every factual claim here comes from the public homepage and is quoted or paraphrased as such. For accounts, billing, support or the current terms, go to hyperagent.com directly rather than trusting a third-party summary like this one.

Does it host the sites it builds?

The site says websites are built and hosted rather than pasted into a document, which reads as hosting included for artifacts made on the platform. It does not spell out custom domains, export paths, or what happens to a hosted page after you cancel, so confirm that before you put anything customer-facing behind it.

What does a fleet of agents mean in practice?

Per the homepage, each agent gets its own computing environment with a real browser and a real shell, and you brief them the way you would brief a new hire. They search, code and build, then deliver into Slack, Telegram, a webhook or a schedule. The copy also claims they keep delivered work updated over time.

Is there a free trial?

Nothing on the homepage mentions a free tier or a trial. The only calls to action are log in and sign up, and cost is communicated through example artifacts rather than a plan table. Check the official site for current signup terms, since access details on new products of this kind change from month to month.

What is the lighter alternative for a one-off build?

When the job is a single site or app rather than a stream of deliverables, Begin.sh takes a prompt or a URL to clone and hands back a static site or Expo app as a downloadable zip. No fleet to manage, no hosting and no backend, which keeps the overhead close to zero for a one-time build.

Want the files, not a fleet?

If what you actually need is a working site or Expo app you can open in your editor, describe it at Begin.sh and download the zip. Point it at an existing URL when that is faster than explaining the layout.
